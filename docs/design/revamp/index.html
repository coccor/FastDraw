Where this stands

Decided on 14 November 2026: the built-in look stays as it is on main. After three rounds, your verdict was that the baseline is still the best. Of the new ideas, Shade is the cleanest and Engraved, its louder sibling, the one closest to it. Those two, with Keys, Offset, Transit and Listing, are kept as candidates for extra selectable styles. Nothing in the product was changed by this exploration: the one build that was made (blue pen, yellow highlighter) was reverted.

Built on 14 November 2026: the six are now real looks, chosen with one line (look shade, look engraved, look keys, look transit, look offset, look listing) or from the editor's Look panel, and shown on the landing page's look switcher. Each is a plain .look file: the renderer learned the drawing they need as look properties any look can use (a hatched shadow, a slab under a box, a second inner rule, a fill out of register, halftone, corner marks, cased and double lines, label plates, a stop marker, striped and sprocketed paper, grain). They were held to three rules while being built:

  • The built-in look and the five older looks are untouched. All 85 example diagrams draw byte for byte what main draws.
  • A diagram laid out for the built-in look fits in every style. The prototypes' larger type was brought back to the built-in sizes (12px titles, 9px second lines, 10px line labels), and all 82 kind examples pass the checker in each style with nothing new reported.
  • Text stays readable on a dark subject. Where a diagram lays its own text over a box that a style fills dark, the text takes that box's light colour.

The prototype pictures further down show the first drawings; the looks as shipped have slightly smaller type than those.

The home page: three directions

The diagrams are settled, so the next surface is fastdraw.dev's home page. Nothing in the product has changed yet; these are prototypes, drawn with the real renderer in the built-in look.

What is wrong with the page today

  • It is 9,650 pixels tall, and almost half of that is a grid of 44 identical cards. The thing a visitor should do (set up their agent) sits about 3,700 pixels down.
  • The hero describes the product instead of showing it. A picture of an editor window and a picture of a terminal sit beside the headline. The one claim nobody else makes, that dragging a box rewrites a line of text, is a small diff tucked under the picture.
  • Three large diagrams follow one another (architecture, the look switcher, the exploded stack), each with its own headline. They make the same point: these are real diagrams.
  • "Why a text file" is four identical cards with an icon each, the most generic section on the page.

What each direction changes

They differ in what the page is, not in colour or type. All three keep the built-in look, Newsreader for headlines, and one indigo.

The brief

Make the UI look good enough that a visitor stops, and then uses the product. Everything is open: the website, the diagram looks, the logo, the editor. FastDraw takes diagram-design a step further: the diagrams can be edited and shared.

Three surfaces share one identity, and a direction has to work on all of them:

  • The diagrams. The built-in look is what every visitor sees first and what every user ships. If the diagram does not make someone think "that is a nice diagram", nothing around it will.
  • The website. Its job is to show the diagram being made and edited, and get the visitor to set up their agent or open the editor.
  • The editor. It should recede: the diagram is the content.

One thing I have not pinned down: what "Editorial" means to you. I have read it as magazine typography: serif headlines, rules, generous white space, one rich colour. If you have a reference in mind (a site, a magazine, a tool's preset called Editorial), it would settle a lot.

What stays fixed in every direction

  • Directions A to D were drawn under your earlier rules: no cream or off-white "paper" grounds, and no black as the highlight. You have since lifted those, and directions E, F and G use what they ruled out.
  • The opening of the website is the product working: the diagram file beside the canvas, where a visitor can drag a box and watch one line of the file change. That part was built and works; only its styling changes with the direction.
  • Style names in the look (focal, store, primary and so on) do not change, so all 44 guides and every existing diagram keep working.
  • Light and dark versions of whatever we choose.

Round three: diagram-design's restraint, with an idea of our own

You said the five styles of round two are worth keeping as styles, but none is "the one", and pointed me at diagram-design. Reading its style guide answered the question I kept asking: "Editorial" is its own word ("Editorial diagrams your designer won't hate"). What makes it good is not a trick of drawing. It is restraint:

  • White-smoke paper, slate ink, hairline outlines, no shadows, small corner radii.
  • One accent, on the one or two things the reader should look at first.
  • A serif title, and italic serif for an editor's asides. Typewriter capitals only for technical labels: ports, protocols, types.
  • Few things on the page. "The highest-quality move is usually deletion."

All five of my round-two styles were louder than this, which is likely why none felt right. The four below keep that restraint (same paper, ink, hairlines, type roles, and the note drawn as an italic aside) and each adds one drawing idea that diagram-design does not have, so the result is ours. The accent here is FastDraw's indigo; the accent is a separate decision.

6. Plates

Every box is a thin plate lying on the page, with a visible edge. Zones are larger plates underneath, so a diagram is a few layers stacked. It borrows from the exploded-view diagrams FastDraw already draws.

How it is drawnA hairline card with a second hairline 5px below it, the gap filled pale grey: the edge of the plate. The subject plate is tinted, with a tinted edge. Stores are short drums. Things outside the system are flat dashed outlines: no plate, because they are not built by us.
What building it takesLow: one extra shape under each box. Lines and layout unchanged.

For: the smallest step from diagram-design that still gives a recognisable silhouette, and it matches the layer and exploded diagrams that are already FastDraw's best-looking kinds. Works at any size. Against: the edge is subtle; at thumbnail size it reads as a heavier bottom border. It is depth, which diagram-design rules out on principle.

7. Brackets

A box is marked at its corners, not fenced in. The page stays open and the text carries the picture.

How it is drawnA white field with four corner marks in ink; no full outline. The subject gets heavier indigo corners and a tint. Stores are grey fields with a rule top and bottom. Zones are larger, lighter corner marks. Each line starts with a small open dot.
What building it takesLow: corner marks in place of outlines, a dot at line starts.

For: the lightest and most airy of all nine; nothing else looks like it. Dense diagrams stay calm because there are far fewer lines on the page. Against: on a white slide the white fields vanish and only the corners remain, which can make boxes hard to find. Shapes other than rectangles (diamonds, cylinders) need their own answer.

8. Shade

A hairline card that casts a shadow made of fine drawn lines. It is the one piece of texture on the page, and it is the Engraved idea from round two reduced to a whisper.

How it is drawnSquare hairline cards. Along the right and bottom edge of each, a 5px band of diagonal hatching. The subject's shadow is hatched in indigo. Zones are fields of wider, fainter hatching inside a hairline, with the name set into the top rule.
What building it takesLow to moderate: a hatched band per box; the hatching already exists in the renderer.

For: looks drawn by hand with a ruling pen, and the most "editorial" of the four. The hatching survives black-and-white printing. Against: fine hatching goes grey at small sizes, and hatched zones behind hatched shadows can get busy in dense diagrams.

9. Tabs

Every box is an index card with a tab that says what it is. Zones are folders holding the cards.

How it is drawnA hairline card with a small tab on its top-left edge. The tab carries the type marker that diagram-design puts in a chip inside the box (API, DATA, EXT). Zones are folders with a larger tab for their name. Stores are grey cards.
What building it takesLow: a tab on the outline. The type marker moves from inside the box to the tab, which frees space in the box.

For: a distinctive silhouette that also does a job: you can read what kind of thing a box is before you read its name. Folders holding cards is an honest picture of grouping. Against: cards without a type get an empty tab or none, so some diagrams look uneven. Tabs need a few pixels of room above every box.

Round two: five styles of our own

These are the styles you asked to keep. You said the first round was small variations on the baseline, and that you want styles as different from each other as Miro is from Excalidraw, without borrowing either. That is right: everything in round one was the same drawing language, outlined boxes joined by thin arrows, in different colours and fonts.

The five below differ in how a diagram is drawn: what a box is made of, what a line is, where depth and texture come from. They are prototypes: the layout is FastDraw's own, taken from the same two files, but each picture comes from a separate throwaway renderer (docs/design/revamp/proto/proto.ts), not from the product. Rough edges are expected; judge the idea.

1. Keys

Every box is a solid key you could press: a face, and a coloured side that shows its depth. Lines are thick cables, and their labels are tags clipped onto them.

How it is drawnRounded faces with a 2px dark outline and a second, darker slab underneath. Stores are pill-shaped keys in green; the subject is a violet key. Zones are shallow trays. Things outside the system are flat dotted outlines, because they are not ours to press. Notes are tilted yellow keys.
What building it takesModerate: a depth slab under each shape, pill labels on lines, thicker line weights. No new layout.

For: tactile and friendly, and it photographs well in a README. The idea of pressing matches a product where you grab and move things. Against: of the five it is the nearest to today's tools (rounded, cheerful, coloured). Dense diagrams get heavy with every box carrying a slab.

2. Offset

Printed in two spot inks, slightly out of register, the way a small press prints a poster. There is no black anywhere: blue is the ink, red is the subject, tints are fields of dots.

How it is drawnBlue outlines with the colour fill shifted a few pixels down and right, as if the second ink missed. Zones and notes are halftone dots, not flat tints. Inks multiply where they overlap. The title is printed twice, red under blue. A fine paper grain over everything.
What building it takesHigher: offset fills, dot patterns, blending between inks and a grain layer. Exports get larger.

For: the most unusual of the five, and nothing in the diagram-tool world looks like it. Very recognisable at thumbnail size. Against: the texture costs legibility on small text and dense tables, and it is the hardest to keep clean across all 44 kinds. Some people will read it as a mistake in the print.

3. Engraved

A plate from an old technical book. Double rules around every box, shadows and curved surfaces shaded with fine cut lines, a tall serif title over a swelled rule, notes in italic. This is the Editorial idea made into a drawing language of its own.

How it is drawnEach box has an outer rule and a hairline inside it, and a shadow made of diagonal hatching along its right and bottom edges. Cylinders are shaded with hatching on their sides, like turned metal. Zones are hatched fields with their name set into the top rule and a square at each corner. The subject is a solid black plate; the main path is a red double line.
What building it takesHigher: double rules, hatch shading for shadows and cylinders, titled borders, double-line arrows.

For: the most beautiful as a picture, and exactly the register you asked for with "Editorial". Looks made by a person with a pen. Against: fine lines need resolution: at small sizes the hatching turns to grey. Paper ground and black plate bring back the two things you had ruled out before. Formal; less at home in a casual README.

4. Transit

A transit map. The lines are the picture: thick coloured routes with sweeping bends, and the boxes are stations along them, with a ring where a route stops.

How it is drawnRoutes are 6 to 9px wide with wide curved corners and a white casing so they cross cleanly. Their names ride on them as coloured plates. Stations are pills with a heavy outline; stores get a second ring; the subject is a solid dark station. Zones are pale fare zones.
What building it takesModerate: wide lines with casings and large corner radii, stop rings at line ends, plate labels. Needs wider gaps between boxes in some guides.

For: puts the flow first, which is what most architecture and process diagrams are about. Bold, clear from across a room, and familiar to everyone without copying any one map. Against: less suited to kinds that are not about flow: tables, charts, matrices. Thick lines crowd when many arrows share a corridor.

5. Listing

The diagram as a line printer would put it on green-bar paper, sprocket holes and all. A FastDraw diagram is a text file; in this style the picture admits it.

How it is drawnEverything sits on a character grid in one typewriter face, names in capitals. Corners are marked with plus signs, the subject is printed in reverse, stores have a double border, zones are shaded with printer characters. Lines turn at square joints.
What building it takesLow to moderate: mostly what the renderer already does, on a grid, plus the paper.

For: the only one that says something true about the product: text in, picture out. Engineers will smile. Against: a novelty for anyone outside engineering, and the green paper is a strong statement on a slide. Capitals in one typeface make long names hard to scan.

Kept for the record. These change colour, type and finish but not the drawing language, which is why they read as the same style.

Baseline: the look on main today

Rounded white cards with two-tone icons, softly tinted zones, slate greys, one indigo. This is the one to beat.

What works: friendly, clear, the icons give it life. What does not: the title and labels are plain sans, so nothing about it is recognisably FastDraw; it could be any modern diagram tool. The website pairs a serif headline with soft cards and coloured window dots, which reads as a template.

What is still open

  • What the revamp should now change, given that the diagrams themselves stay: the website, the editor, the logo, or none of them.
  • Whether and when the extra styles get built. Shade is the cheapest (the hatching already exists in the renderer). Engraved, Keys, Transit, Offset and Listing each need the renderer to learn something new.
